# Sham and blinding

Stimulation has side effects people can feel or hear (scalp sensation,
coil click, muscle twitch, auditory tone), so a naive sham is easy to
detect. Design the sham to match what participants perceive, then
measure whether blinding held, and analyse with that in mind.

## 1. Choose the control

| Method | Sham options | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| TMS | sham coil with matched click and scalp stimulation; active control site | twitches and sensations differ at high intensity |
| tDCS/tACS | ramp up and down over about 30 s then off (Gandiga et al. 2006); active low-dose sham | sensations at onset; skin redness; experienced participants |
| TUS | sound-matched sham, auditory masking | audible PRF tone evokes responses by itself |
| DBS/VNS | stimulation off or subtherapeutic; randomised order | paraesthesia reveals condition; carry-over effects |
| neurofeedback | feedback from another person's or a non-target signal | effort and reward also differ |

Prefer an active control (real stimulation of a non-target area or
parameter) when the sham cannot match sensations.

## 2. Keep operators blind too

Use stimulators with code-based or study modes so the person running the
session does not know the condition; separate the person who randomises
from the person who assesses outcomes.

## 3. Measure blinding

After each session (or at the end) ask participants, and operators,
which condition they think it was and how confident they are. Compute a
blinding index per arm (Bang et al. 2004 is commonly used; it separates
correct guessing from guessing "active" for everyone). Report guesses by
arm, and side effects by arm.

## 4. Analyse with blinding in mind

If blinding failed, test whether the effect remains after adjusting for
perceived condition, and report it as a limitation.

## Output

Control design and rationale, operator blinding, questionnaire, blinding
index results, side effects by arm, and the analysis adjustment.
